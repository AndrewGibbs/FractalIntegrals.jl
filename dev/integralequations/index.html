Integral Equations

In the previous section we saw how to approximate integrals on fractals. Now, we turn our attention to solving integral equations on fractals. Many constant coefficient PDEs can be reformulated as integral equations, and in some circumstances, the integral equation is easier to solve than the original PDE.

A brief introduction to integral equations on fractals

When solving problems on fractals, a common approach is to approximate the fractal $\Gamma$ by a shape that $\tilde{\Gamma}\approx\Gamma$ that is sufficiently detailed, but non-fractal, and instead solve the same problem on $\tilde{\Gamma}$. This is appealing because it enables the use of standard methods and/or software. However, this geometric approximation introduces an error that is difficult to quantify.

Recently, in the context of integral equations [8, 9], techniques have been developed for solving problems on $\Gamma$, without the need for a geometrical approximation $\tilde{\Gamma}$.

For our purposes, we consider integral equations of the form:

\[(\lambda~\mathcal{I} + \mathcal{K})~v= f,\quad\text{on }\Gamma,\]

where $v$ is the unknown solution, $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$, $\mathcal{I}$ is the identity operator, $f$ is known data and $\mathcal{K}$ is an integral operator of the form

\[\mathcal{K}~\phi~(x)= \int_\Gamma K(x,y)~\phi(y)~ \mathrm{d}\mu(y),\quad x\in\Gamma,\]

where $K$ is a known integral kernel.

For our purposes, we make interpret $\Gamma$ as an attractor, or a union of attractors. Typically, when one has solved for $v$, a potential operator is used to obtain a solution to the equivalent PDE

\[\mathcal{V} v(x) = \int_\Gamma ~V(x,y)~v(y)~ \mathrm{d}\mu(y), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}^N.\]

Classification

There are two key classes of integral equations, and this distinction is particularly significant in the fractal setting:

  • 1st kind: When $\lambda=0$, equivalently, the identity term is removed.
  • 2nd kind: When $\lambda\neq0$.

As mentioned in (geometry.md#measures), for many attractors $\Gamma$, we do not know $\mathcal{H}^d(\Gamma)$. In this case, we cannot work with $\mu=\mathcal{H}^d$ directly, instead we must work with $\tilde\mu(x) = \mathcal{H}^d(x)/\mathcal{H}^d(\Gamma)$. Hence, if the integral equation is defined with respect to Hausdorff measure, we can (usually) only solve a related integral equation with respect to $\tilde\mu$.

In the case of first kind integral equations, the solution to the related problem $\tilde v=v\cdot \mathcal{H}^d(\Gamma)$. If the related potential operator $\tilde{\mathcal{V}}$ is defined analogously with respect to $\tilde\mu$, then $\mathcal{V}v = \tilde{\mathcal{V}} \tilde v$; in other words, the solution of the underlying PDE is unaffected by our scaling of the measure, thus we do not need to know $\mathcal{H}^d(\Gamma)$.

In the case of second kind integral equations, we are not so fortunate: $\tilde v \neq v\cdot \mathcal{H}^d(\Gamma)$. Therefore, when posing a second-kind integral equation, for meaningful results, it is essential that the suppmeasure parameter, mathematically defined as $\mu(\Gamma)$, is known and included in the definition of the measure. We note that for the case when $\Gamma$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^N$ with a fractal boundary, for example the Koch Snowflake, the $N$-dimensional Lebesgue measure is usually known.

Seperable integral operatorss

Even when the domain is smooth, approximating singular integrals arising in integral equation discretisations can cause headaches.

On fractal domains, there is a significantly smaller family of kernels $K$ for which integral equations can solved. This close-knit family of kernels can be described as follows:

\[K(x,y) = K_\mathrm{smooth}(x,y) + c\cdot K_s(x,y) \]

where $K_\mathrm{smooth}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and

\[K_s(x,y) := \left\{\begin{array}{cc} \log|x-y|,& s=0,\\ |x-y|^{-s},& s>0, \end{array}\right.,\]

for some $s\geq0$. Because our approximation to the unknown $\phi$ is piecewise constant, we can write

\[\int_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}}\int_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}} K(x,y) ~ \phi(y) ~ \mathrm{d}\mu(x) \mathrm{d}\mu(y) = \phi|_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}}\left[\int_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}}\int_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}} K_\mathrm{smooth}(x,y) ~ \mathrm{d}\mu(x) \mathrm{d}\mu(y) + c\cdot\mathcal{E}[\mu|_{\Gamma_\mathbf{m}}]\right].\]

The first integral is evaluated by standard fractal quadrature rules. The second integral is evaluated by the singular algorithm.

FractalIntegrals.IdentityOperator — Type
IdentityOperator{M} <: FractalOperator{M}

Represents the identity operator $\mathcal{I}$ in an integral equation on a fractal measure support $\Gamma$, defined by

\[\mathcal{I}\,\phi\,(x) = \phi(x), \quad x \in \Gamma.\]

This is typically used as part of a second-kind integral equation of the form

\[(\lambda\,\mathcal{I} + \mathcal{K})\,v = f, \quad \text{on } \Gamma,\]

where $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and $\mathcal{K}$ is an IntegralOperator.

Fields

  • measure :: M: The invariant measure $\mu$ supported on $\Gamma$.

Examples

Γ = getfractal("cantorset")
μ = HausdorffMeasure(Γ)
I = IdentityOperator(μ)
source
FractalIntegrals.SingularIntegralOperator — Type
SingularIntegralOperator{M, F, Z} <: AbstractSingularIntegralOperator{M, Z}

An integral operator with a purely singular kernel, i.e. with no additional smooth part. The kernel takes the form

\[\mathcal{K}\,\phi\,(x) = \int_\Gamma K(x,y)\,\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y), \quad x \in \Gamma,\]

where $K$ is singular (e.g. a Laplace-type energy kernel). Unlike SeparableIntegralOperator, no kernel splitting is assumed; the entire kernel is treated as singular.

Fields

  • measure :: M: The invariant measure $\mu$ supported on $\Gamma$.
  • kernel :: F: The singular kernel function $K(x,y)$.
  • symmetric :: Bool: Whether the operator is self-adjoint.
source
FractalIntegrals.SeparableIntegralOperator — Type
SeparableIntegralOperator{M, F1, F2, S, Z} <: AbstractSeparableIntegralOperator{M, Z}

An integral operator whose kernel splits into a singular part and a smooth (Lipschitz) part, of the form

\[K(x,y) = K_{\mathrm{smooth}}(x,y) + c \cdot K_s(x,y),\]

where $K_{\mathrm{smooth}} \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and

\[K_s(x,y) := \begin{cases} \log|x-y|, & s=0, \\ |x-y|^{-s}, & s>0, \end{cases}\]

for some $s \geq 0$. This decomposition enables efficient numerical treatment: the smooth part is integrated by standard fractal quadrature rules, and the singular part is handled by a dedicated singular integration algorithm.

This structure is appropriate for Laplace-type kernels (no oscillation). For oscillatory kernels (e.g. Helmholtz), see OscillatorySeparableIntegralOperator. For a purely singular kernel with no smooth part, see SingularIntegralOperator.

Fields

  • measure :: M: The invariant measure $\mu$ supported on $\Gamma$.
  • kernel :: F1: The full kernel function $K(x,y)$.
  • lipschitzpart :: F2: The smooth part $K_{\mathrm{smooth}}(x,y)$ of the kernel.
  • s :: S: The singularity exponent ($s=0$ for logarithmic, $s>0$ for algebraic).
  • singularconst :: Z: The constant $c$ scaling the singular part.
  • symmetric :: Bool: Whether the operator is self-adjoint.

Operator presets

The following preset constructors build the most common integral operators used in acoustic scattering problems.

FractalIntegrals.singlelayer_operator_laplace — Function
singlelayer_operator_laplace(μ::AbstractInvariantMeasure; ambient_dimension)
singlelayer_operator_laplace(Γ::AbstractAttractor; ambient_dimension)
singlelayer_operator_laplace(μple::Tuple; ambient_dimension)

Construct the Laplace single-layer integral operator on a fractal measure support $\Gamma$, defined by

\[\mathcal{K}\,\phi\,(x) = \int_\Gamma \Phi(x,y)\,\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y), \quad x \in \Gamma,\]

where $\Phi$ is the free-space Green's function of the Laplacian:

  • 2D: $\Phi(x,y) = -\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|$
  • 3D: $\Phi(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{4\pi}|x-y|^{-1}$

The kernel is decomposed into singular and smooth parts as a SeparableIntegralOperator, enabling efficient quadrature on fractal domains.

When a Tuple of measures (or attractors) is passed, a BlockOperator is returned representing the operator on the union of supports.

Arguments

  • μ: An AbstractInvariantMeasure supported on $\Gamma$.
  • Γ: An AbstractAttractor (uses Hausdorff measure by default).
  • μple: A Tuple of measures for a multi-component domain.
  • ambient_dimension: The ambient space dimension (default: inferred from μ). Note: ambient_dimension=1 is not valid and will be promoted to 2.

Examples

Γ = getfractal("cantorset")
K = singlelayer_operator_laplace(Γ)

FractalIntegrals.singlelayer_operator_helmholtz — Function
singlelayer_operator_helmholtz(μ::AbstractInvariantMeasure, k; ambient_dimension)
singlelayer_operator_helmholtz(Γ::AbstractAttractor, k; ambient_dimension)
singlelayer_operator_helmholtz(μple::Tuple, k; ambient_dimension)

Construct the Helmholtz single-layer integral operator with wavenumber k on a fractal measure support $\Gamma$, defined by

\[\mathcal{K}\,\phi\,(x) = \int_\Gamma \Phi_k(x,y)\,\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y), \quad x \in \Gamma,\]

where $\Phi_k$ is the free-space Green's function of the Helmholtz equation $(\Delta + k^2)u = 0$:

  • 2D: $\Phi_k(x,y) = \tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{4} H_0^{(1)}(k|x-y|)$
  • 3D: $\Phi_k(x,y) = \tfrac{e^{\mathrm{i}k|x-y|}}{4\pi|x-y|}$

The oscillatory kernel is decomposed into a log-singular (2D) or $1/r$-singular (3D) part and a smooth Lipschitz remainder, stored as an OscillatorySeparableIntegralOperator.

When a Tuple of measures (or attractors) is passed, a BlockOperator is returned representing the operator on the union of supports.

Arguments

  • μ: An AbstractInvariantMeasure supported on $\Gamma$.
  • Γ: An AbstractAttractor (uses Hausdorff measure by default).
  • μple: A Tuple of measures for a multi-component domain.
  • k: The wavenumber $k > 0$.
  • ambient_dimension: The ambient space dimension (default: inferred from μ). Note: ambient_dimension=1 is not valid and will be promoted to 2.

Examples

Γ = getfractal("cantordust")
k = 10.0
K = singlelayer_operator_helmholtz(Γ, k)
